Rotten rulers, poisoned mushrooms, a horse who nearly got a top job, and a man who taxed toilets. Everything your teacher left out. Twenty-three rulers, each buried under a coin with their face on it. Rub the coin clean and the ruler comes with it: how they got the job, what they got up to, and how it ended (badly, usually).

Taking stock

Your hoard

A hoard is what numismatists call a pile of old coins found together, usually buried by someone who meant to come back for it and never did. Worth is counted in sestertii, the coin Romans did their sums in: one aureus is 100, one denarius is 4. In Augustus's day a legionary earned about 900 a year (1,200 after Domitian), so check how many years of soldiering you are holding.

Why learn this?

Rome ran the world for five hundred years. Then it ran out of luck.

Because it is still here.July and August are a dictator and an emperor, Julius and Augustus. The calendar on your wall is Caesar's. Senators, census, capitol, candidate: all Roman words, still doing Roman jobs.
Because it is the best gossip ever written down.Suetonius, Tacitus and Dio were there, or nearly, and they wrote down everything: the horse, the mushrooms, the seashells. Some of it is even true.
Because it shows how power actually works.Who picks the ruler? The Senate said it did. The bodyguards knew better. Watch who gets paid, and you can predict every murder on this page.
Because the good ones are the surprise.A long run of emperors chosen for being good at the job, not just for being somebody's son. Steady government for decades. Peace everywhere? Absolutely not. Then a son got the job, and look what happened.
emperorEM-per-erthe one person in charge of the whole Roman world. The word comes from imperator, which just meant "commander". They never said "king". Kings had been thrown out, and everyone pretended that still counted.
SPQRess-pee-kew-ARSenatus Populusque Romanus: "the Senate and People of Rome". The official name of the Roman state, stamped on coins and carved on buildings (and, people like to think, carried on the legions' flags). The emperors kept using it so everyone could pretend the Senate and People were still in charge. Rome still puts it on its manhole covers.
SenateSEN-itRome's council of rich old men, six hundred of them in togas. Under the emperors they still met, still voted, still gave speeches, and still did exactly what they were told. Mostly.
praetorianpry-TOR-ee-unthe emperor's own bodyguards, thousands of them, living in Rome. Paid to protect him. They murdered Caligula, picked Claudius, sold the throne to Otho and killed Elagabalus. With friends like these.
numismaticsnoo-miz-MAT-iksthe study of coins. Historians love Roman coins because every emperor put his own face on them, with his name round the edge, often with the year. A coin is a tiny official portrait that cannot lie about who was in charge. Rub one clean and you are doing numismatics.
aureusAW-ree-usRome's gold coin, worth 25 denarii: over a month's pay for a soldier. Plural: aurei. Most of the coins on this page are aurei. Constantine's is a solidus, the slimmer gold coin he invented, which lasted 700 years.
denariusdih-NAIR-ee-usRome's silver coin, a day's pay for a working man. Plural: denarii. Caesar, Tiberius, Nero and Agrippina are on denarii here. The Spanish word for money, dinero, is this word, 2,000 years on.
sestertiussess-TER-shusa big brass coin, four to the denarius, 100 to the aureus. Plural: sestertii. The one Romans counted prices in: Vitellius's fish dish, Commodus's appearance fee. Trajan's coin here is probably one.
dynastyDY-nuh-steea run of rulers from the same family. The colours on this page are dynasties. When the colour changes, somebody with no claim at all has just won a war.
